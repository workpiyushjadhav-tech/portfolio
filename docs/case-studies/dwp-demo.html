DWP Bereavement Redesign

When interviewing recently bereaved people wasn’t possible without ethical risk, I changed our research method. What we found led to a service that reaches people first, instead of waiting to be found.

The statement

Bereaved people have three months to claim the full Bereavement Support Payment. I couldn’t find one official touchpoint that tells them.

  • 4+ departmentsDWP, HMRC, local councils and the NHS, each with its own rules and deadlines.
  • 3 monthsTo claim the payment in full. Later claims, up to 21 months, get less.
  • 0 front doorsNo single service that tells people everything they qualify for.

01 · The problem

Three months, four departments, no front door

When someone in the UK loses a partner, they may be owed support from DWP, HMRC, their local council and the NHS. Each has its own rules, forms and deadlines. No single service tells them what they qualify for.

The deadline that matters most is easy to miss. The Bereavement Support Payment (BSP) has to be claimed within 3 months for the full amount. Claims up to 21 months after the death get fewer payments. I found no government message that tells people this, so finding out is left to them, at the moment they’re least able to look.

Help does exist, but in the wrong places. Tell Us Once is offered when a death is registered, yet it’s underused. Registrars, GPs and funeral directors are trusted at exactly the right moment, but they don’t have the tools to point people to the right support.

One of nine secondary research findings

High satisfaction scores in existing data only count the people who made it through. Those who never reached the service don’t show up at all.

“Users switch between websites, helplines and offline services with little continuity across channels. Vulnerable groups (digitally excluded, low-literacy, disabled) face the greatest barriers at the worst possible moment.”

Secondary research synthesis, UK bereavement service landscape

The brief

Get bereaved people the right information at the right time. The service should reach out first.

02 · The pivot

The interviews we chose not to run

The obvious first step was to interview people who had recently lost a partner. In five weeks, that wasn’t possible to do well.

  1. What I discovered

    Recruiting recently bereaved people in five weeks raised serious ethical concerns. A rushed interview with someone in grief risks harming them, and gives weak data.

  2. What I decided

    I proposed a different method to the team: a large-scale analysis of what bereaved people had already written in public. Then I led it.

  3. What I designed

    A dataset of 636 posts from three Reddit communities, analysed with Braun & Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis. Public posts only, usernames removed, ethics documentation in place.

  4. What it cost

    People who post online aren’t a fair sample of all bereaved people. I treat the findings as directional, not proof.

It gave us more material than five weeks of recruiting ever could have, and nobody in grief was asked to relive it for our research.

03 · The posts

What 636 posts said, and what the counts hid

People asked for advice, not information. The most common keyword across all three communities was “advice”. My reading: people were turning to strangers online because official sources weren’t telling them what to do next. The system had plenty of information. What it lacked was direction.

What I learned

How often something comes up isn’t the same as how much it matters. I almost skipped this theme because it was the smallest.

04 · The people

Sarah and James

To see where the system fails in practice, I built two composite personas from the stories in the posts and mapped their journeys. The journeys showed failure points the theme counts had only hinted at.

“I don’t even know what I don’t know. I just need someone to tell me what I’m supposed to do next.”

Sarah needs a clear starting point instead of ten options, plain language, deadline reminders, and to know she’s doing things right. She screenshots pages, starts forms then stops, and searches late at night once the children are asleep.

“I just want one place that tells me what to do step by step, not ten pages telling me everything at once.”

James needs one step-by-step place, plain English, a clear “do this now, do this later”, and progress he can come back to. He opens several GOV.UK pages and doesn’t know which to start with, avoids phoning organisations, and is managing grief and urgent admin on his own.

Sarah’s journey, in five feelings
  1. Acute shockNumb
  2. AdminOverwhelmed
  3. DWP contactFrustrated
  4. BSP paymentResigned
  5. Slow resolutionDepleted
Where it broke
  • No signposting when she registered the death.
  • A 35-minute DWP hold, asking for documents she didn’t have to hand.
  • A BSP payment arriving with no message to explain it.
The one that cost her money

She found out about the council tax single occupancy discount three months late, after the full backdating window had closed.

Sarah and James are composites built from stories in the research. Their quotes are written to represent those stories, not taken word for word.

05 · The map

The map that changed my answer

Before this step, I would have proposed a redesigned GOV.UK page. It’s the obvious fix: put everything in one place. Then I mapped every touchpoint a bereaved person could pass through before reaching BSP.

  1. What I discovered

    No single “start here” point anywhere in the system, and no official touchpoint that mentions the 3-month window. The rules were spread across at least four agencies.

  2. What I decided

    A better page only helps people who already know to look for it. The service had to find people, at more than one moment.

  3. What I designed

    The three-layer structure came straight out of this map.

  4. What I learned

    Putting the whole system on one surface showed a gap that no single screen could fix.

Looking further afield

I reviewed bereavement services in four countries and services from other sectors that solve a similar problem. One pattern held across the strongest ones: the service makes first contact instead of waiting to be found.

Macmillan Cancer Support

Breaks a complicated process into clear stages.

NHS Symptom Checker

Asks a few questions, then gives personal next steps.

e-Estonia

You give your details once and never repeat them.

How might we

make sure bereaved people get the right information, without having to search for it, in a way they can take in while grieving?

06 · The choice

Why one fix wasn’t enough

Bereaved people aren’t one group. An older widow, a teenager like James and a working parent like Sarah meet the system at different moments. We compared four directions.

  • Redesign one GOV.UK pageHelps confident users and misses everyone who never reaches GOV.UK.
  • Improve the phone helplineTrusted, but people still have to know to call.
  • A tool for professionals onlyA registrar sees one moment. People between touchpoints get nothing.
  • ChosenThree layers working togetherEach layer covers a moment the others miss.

What it cost

More moving parts, and none of the three layers has been tested with users yet.

07 · The service

Three ways to reach someone

The same principle runs through all three layers: the service makes contact first. No dead ends. No navigating without direction. No deadline without a clear way to act on it.

Layer 01

A letter that arrives first

Straight after registration

Tell Us Once triggers an automatic, personal letter (and a text where a number is held). It lists 3 to 5 entitlements by name, each with its deadline worked out and one way to get in touch.

  • Designed to look official and time-sensitive, not like junk mail (not tested)
  • BSL, translated and large print formats
  • Built on Tell Us Once instead of new systems (not yet confirmed it can send a letter)
Layer 02

A five-question checker

Whenever someone looks on GOV.UK

No account needed. Five questions cover the relationship, National Insurance record, children, current benefits and date of death. The result is a ranked checklist with deadline countdowns and a direct link to start each claim.

  • Shows nothing until it’s needed
  • Every path ends in an action or a person to contact
  • Can be printed, saved or used through a phone relay service
Layer 03

A dashboard for trusted professionals

At the registrar, GP or funeral director

One trained registrar sees everyone who registers a death in their office. The dashboard takes the type of client and date of death, and shows likely entitlements across all four departments, with deadline warnings.

  • People already trust these professionals
  • All four departments in one view
  • Adds to existing contact points, not new ones
From structure to screens

I designed the wireframes and information architecture for all three layers. The prototype uses GOV.UK Design System components and patterns, so it looks and behaves like a real GOV.UK service, and I designed it to WCAG 2.1 AA.

08 · The unknowns

What only bereaved people can tell us

There’s no measured impact yet. The solution has never been in front of a bereaved person. Here’s what the evidence supports, and what it doesn’t.

What the evidence supports
  • The problem

    13,000+ coded sentences from public posts, eight themes and 41 codes. Reddit users aren’t a representative sample.

  • The direction

    Strong services in other countries and sectors make first contact. That’s precedent, not proof it works here.

  • Tell Us Once exists and is underused

    As far as I checked. Whether it can trigger the letter is untested.

  • What it doesn’t support yet

    Usability testing with bereaved people. Not done, and needed before any launch.

Two questions only real testing can answer
01Does the letter actually get read?

Risk: older bereaved spouses are the most likely to receive it, and the least likely to recognise an unfamiliar DWP letter as important.

Next step: test with people aged 60+, people with low digital confidence, and people who didn’t manage the household accounts.

02Do five questions cover the unusual cases?

Risk: partners who lived together but weren’t married, deaths abroad and complicated older benefits weren’t fully mapped.

Next step: a sprint testing the checker against unusual relationship statuses and mixed benefit histories.

09 · Reflection

What this project taught me

  • Changing the method was the right call

    I chose public posts over rushed interviews with people in grief. It gave us more material than five weeks of recruiting could, and it’s the decision I’m most proud of. The cost is that the sample isn’t representative, and I say so wherever the findings appear.

  • My first idea was the obvious one, and it was wrong

    Before the mapping, I’d have redesigned a single GOV.UK page. The map showed that a page only helps people who already know to look.

  • Counting and reading are different jobs

    The smallest theme shaped the whole service. That took judgement beyond a count. It’s my reading, not a measured result, so I’ve shown the numbers behind it and let the reader judge.

  • What’s still open

    No live testing. Unmarried partners and deaths abroad are a known gap in the checker. And I haven’t confirmed Tell Us Once can trigger the letter.
