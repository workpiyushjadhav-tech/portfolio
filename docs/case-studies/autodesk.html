Challenge

The Challenge

Autodesk Construction Cloud has Change Analysis and Clash Analysis, both critical to BIM workflows. They sit in entirely different parts of the platform, yet in users’ real work they are closely related.

Change Analysis

What was added, removed or modified

What
Tracks changes to 3D building model objects across versions: what moved, who moved it and when.
Clash Analysis

Where objects overlap in space

What
Identifies when model objects physically overlap, a conflict that must be resolved before a building can be constructed.

What users want. When a clash is found, they want to trace it back to the change that introduced it. Resolving it means changing one or both objects, so the brief asked whether those changes could be suggested, or recorded against the clash as history.

Approach

My Approach

Three phases across the 6-week sprint: Discovery, Ideation, Evaluation. Autodesk supplied background data, insights, personas and excerpts from stakeholder and customer interviews (not shared here). We added public data, a heuristic evaluation and benchmarking, and analysed the interview excerpts.

01

Heuristic evaluation and large-scale content analysis

We ran a heuristic evaluation of the platform’s workflows against Nielsen’s 10 heuristics. Alongside it, we collected 25,000+ public comments about ACC, Clash and Change Analysis, model coordination, design collaboration and Navisworks.

  • AI sped up the first pass by helping us decide which comments were worth coding, but it made mistakes when identifying codes, so the team reviewed and aligned every code by hand. We then filtered manually in Excel, ending with 160 actionable insights. Codes and themes were defined by the team, using thematic and sentiment analysis.
  • Key finding from the heuristic evaluation: an inconsistent UI and limited feedback make it hard to connect model changes with clashes, track them over time, and move between workflows. It was also unclear who made a change, where it came from and who should resolve the issue.
02

Thematic analysis: five themes, two dominant

Codes were grouped into five overarching themes, and two became the focus.

  • Positives
  • Pain Points and Frustrations
  • Manual Workarounds
  • Workflow Challenges
  • Visibility Gaps
03

Benchmark analysis and analogous inspiration

We benchmarked platforms directly and indirectly related to the problem space to find best practice and limitations. One example stood out: Navisworks tracks clashes through a clear status lifecycle, from New through to Resolved. That gives teams a shared view of what has been dealt with and what still needs attention. Analogous inspiration surfaced useful patterns from outside the BIM field.

NewResolved

The research pointed to a fragmented coordination ecosystem: no data continuity between detecting a clash and tracking the issue, and high cognitive load from moving across multiple tools.

Problem statement

Users face a critical visibility gap. There is no functional bridge between “what changed” and “what broke”.

Teams can’t establish what caused a new issue, so they reconcile manually, which raises the risk of oversight, weakens collaborative reviews and obscures the audit trail behind decisions.

From research to direction. HMW questions came out of the thematic analysis, were prioritised on an impact × frequency matrix, then formalised into a MoSCoW requirements set.

  • Connect clash to changeHMW trace a detected clash back to the specific change that introduced it, in one workflow rather than two tools?
  • Reduce cognitive overheadHMW reduce the effort of navigating large volumes of clash data without enough contextual control?
  • Proactive over reactiveHMW move teams from discovering clashes after they occur to anticipating them?
  • Create an audit trailHMW record how clashes were resolved and surface that history when similar conflicts arise in later model iterations?

Solution

The Solution

We built the prototype through iterative wireframing in the Ideation and Evaluation phases, using the problem statement, the personas and the interview insights. It has two contributions.

01

An object and filter-based approach to clash and change analysis

The core friction was cognitive overhead: dense lists of clashes with little contextual control. The prototype introduced an object and filter-based interaction model so users can isolate, group and prioritise clashes by parameters such as discipline, severity or model element type, giving them more control over what they review and cutting the noise in clash review sessions.

Not tested with end users
02

Predictive clash forecasting

The second contribution moves teams from reactive to proactive. Rather than only surfacing clashes after they occur, the prototype explored a forecasting model that could signal likely conflict zones from patterns in model changes over time. The research showed late discovery of clashes causes delays and rework. It didn’t show that prediction is possible, so this is an exploration, not a validated model.

Exploration, not validated
How the two connect

Clash and change, in one trace

What
The core issue was that clash and change analysis weren’t associated in the product. When one team’s change causes a clash for another team, the user should be able to see who made it. The filters show who made each change and when, so the user can contact the right team to fix it, and forecasting aims to flag likely conflicts before they happen so teams can avoid them. Together they’re designed to let a user trace from a clash back to its change, or from a change forward to the clashes it caused.
Status
This is design intent, not tested with end users.

Impact

Measurable Impact

There’s no measured impact yet. The prototype hasn’t been tested with end users, so this section sets out what the evidence supports and what it doesn’t.

What the evidence supports

  • Problem direction: Workflow Challenges and Visibility Gaps were the two key themes across 25,000+ public comments from several sources.
  • Benchmark pattern: Navisworks’ clash status lifecycle is an existing model, so the direction has precedent.
  • Interview excerpts from Autodesk: Analysed to cross-reference the public data (findings not shared here).
  • Developer feedback: A developer who had worked on the project previously gave formal, technical feedback on our ideas, and background to base them on. That’s expert input, not end-user testing.

What it doesn’t support yet

  • End-user testing: Not conducted. Required before any deployment decision.

Limits

What we can’t claim yet

Three limitations, each with a next step.

Limitation 01

Data collection scope

Gap
Public datasets skew toward frustrated users. Power users, satisfied users and those who simply get on with the tool are largely invisible. Findings are directionally valid but need validating against a broader sample.
Next step
Structured research with a representative cross-section of ACC users across disciplines and project types.
Limitation 02

No end-user testing

Gap
The prototype wasn’t tested with real BIM Managers or Engineers. Instead, a developer who had worked on the project previously reviewed it (see above). Given the domain complexity (high-stakes construction workflows), that’s a significant gap that stakeholder input can’t substitute for.
Next step
A task-based usability study with BIM Managers and Engineers using the integrated prototype in a real project context.
Limitation 03

Generalisability of platform findings

Gap
Findings from the platform audit may not generalise beyond the specific workflows studied. ACC serves a wide range of construction disciplines with different coordination priorities.
Next step
Validate the forecasting model against historical clash and change data from live ACC projects across multiple disciplines.

Takeaways

Key takeaways: decisions and what they cost

  • AI to narrow, people to judge.

    Reading 25,000+ comments line by line wasn’t feasible in six weeks. The AI-assisted first filter made it workable, but it made coding mistakes, so people made the final calls. The cost is that the first filter shaped what we saw.

  • Pushing past the brief.

    The brief asked how to integrate two views. Forecasting was our attempt at a different mental model, proactive rather than reactive, and it stays an exploration.

  • A benchmark as a design anchor.

    An existing clash status lifecycle gave us a pattern to learn from instead of designing resolution tracking from scratch.

Unresolved

What’s unresolved

Role-based view logic. BIM Managers, Engineers and Architects have different needs when reviewing clashes and changes. The prototype addressed integration, but the permission model and role-based views would need a further sprint.

The brief asked for two levels of work: workflow design (when and where to show clash and change analysis, and to which users) and UI design (how the two sets of results interact in the interface). We worked on both.
